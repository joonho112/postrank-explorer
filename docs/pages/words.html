Terminology and notation

Targets, reporting losses and computation scope

Definitions of posterior quantities, common grades, multidomain profiles, disclosure, error measures and report families.

An Alabama county and a California school are both units, but their reports compare different outcomes. The definitions below specify what is compared, how the posterior enters the comparison, and which reports can be chosen.

Targets and posterior uncertainty

Unit; criterion k
A unit is a firm, school, site, or other entity included in the comparison. A criterion is an outcome or other target used to evaluate the report. For example, income and incarceration are two criteria measured for the same counties. Examples: Multiple criteria · Supplied summaries
θi
The target for unit i, coded so that higher values receive higher reporting priority. The application states what higher priority means for that target. Examples: Two-unit comparison · Alabama counties
Posterior
The distribution of target values given the data and model. Posterior risk averages the loss over this distribution. Calibration concerns how those probabilities agree with outcomes under a specified evaluation law or in new data. Examples: Posterior shrinkage · Posterior inputs
πij
The posterior probability of a direction such as Pr⁡(θi>θj). This calculation uses the joint distribution of the two targets because the event depends on their difference. Examples: Direction probabilities · Interval overlap
eij(k)
The probability that stating i above j is wrong on criterion k. Under the zero-margin weak-error convention, equality makes either strict direction wrong. If equality has positive probability, the two directional errors sum to more than one. Each example specifies the event and any comparison margin. Examples: Pairwise decisions · Posterior equality
Marginal interval
An interval containing a specified share of the posterior distribution for one target. A 90% interval for each of two schools gives a probability statement about each school individually. The probability that one school’s target exceeds the other’s uses their joint posterior. Example: Report-card summaries
Expected rank
The posterior average position of a unit under a specified rank and tie convention. Ordering these averages gives one possible ranking and can define a fixed-order report family. Example: Alternative ranking summaries

Grade reports, disclosure and error

Common-grade report g (P1)
An ordered grouping of the units, with grade 1 at the high-priority end. The report states a direction between grades. Within a grade it leaves the comparison unstated. Using the report to allocate places or resources requires a separate rule. Examples: Report construction · Allocation objectives
n, M
The number of included units and their M=n(n−1)/2 unordered pairs. Each cross-grade pair contributes one stated direction. Example: Report-family counts
N, s=N/M
The number of stated pairs and the disclosure share. The remaining M−N pairs are withheld. Example: Disclosure from tier sizes
Ek
The posterior expected number of wrong stated directions on criterion k, obtained by summing directional error probabilities over the stated pairs. Joint correctness is a separate probability. Example: Expected and realized errors
Ek/N
The expected wrong share among stated pairs of a fixed report. It is NA: no disclosure when N=0. Example: Error-rate denominators
DRk=Ek/M
The expected wrong count per possible pair. Withholding can reduce this rate by reducing the set of stated directions. The criterion subscript is usually omitted in single-criterion examples. Example: Error-rate denominators
τ
The cost per withheld pair when a wrong stated direction costs one. Weighted risk is Rτ=(Ew+τ(M−N))/M. Example: Reporting risk
λ, ϕ
KRW’s reward for a correct direction and the informed share in their stipulated audience model. The conversion is τ=λ/(1+λ) and ϕ=1−2τ. The positive-cost audience range is 0<λ≤1, 0<τ≤1/2 and 0≤ϕ<1. Equivalence of optimal reports requires the stated common-posterior, tie-free and common-family conditions. Examples: Loss-scale conversion · Equivalent reporting losses

A loss-minimizing report satisfies Ew≤τN when the family includes the report that withholds every comparison. The inequality follows by comparing the chosen report’s loss with that benchmark. A grade report chosen by a different rule may exceed the benchmark.

Report families and comparisons

Family; grade cap
The set of reports considered in the search. A grade cap limits the number of grades. A fixed order limits grade boundaries to cuts along that order, so the best report among those cuts may differ from the best report over all orders. Some fixed orders also keep equal scores together. Examples: Report families · Computation methods
Frontier
The lower convex boundary of expected wrong counts plotted against disclosure for a specified report family. A point on an edge may require randomizing between complete reports. Two reports can minimize risk at the same τ while stating different numbers of pairs. Examples: Error–disclosure frontier · Comparison constraints
ψ
The probability of choosing the report that states more pairs in a mixture of two frontier reports. Expected disclosure averages the two pair counts using the mixing probabilities. An individual draw produces one of the two complete reports. Example: Mixtures of reports
wk, Ew=∑kwkEk
Nonnegative criterion error weights summing to one, and the weighted expected wrong count. These weights apply to directional losses. Coefficients of a scalar composite target have a separate role. Examples: Common grades · Composite targets
qk
An expected-error budget, expressed in count form as Ek≤qkN. With no stated directions both sides are zero, although the error share is undefined. Some applications use budgets only to evaluate the displayed report; others use them to restrict selection. Examples: Budget-constrained families · Case-report checks
Δk
The risk of the common report on criterion k minus the minimum risk for that criterion under the same loss and report family. If the exact minimum is unknown, bounds on it give an interval for the excess risk. Changing the loss or family changes the benchmark. Example: Criterion-specific excess risk
Selection belief; evaluation law
The distribution used to select a report and the distribution used to evaluate that fixed report. Using a different evaluation law can change expected loss for the same grade vector. Examples: Alternative evaluation laws · Selection-draw evaluation
Joint correctness
The probability that all stated directions are correct together. Pairwise probabilities and expected wrong counts alone do not determine this event probability. Composite targets similarly require the relevant joint-law information. Example: Limits of pairwise probabilities
Simulation error
Numerical uncertainty due to a finite set of posterior draws. Independent draws and dependent chains require different variance calculations and diagnostics. Increasing the number of draws reduces numerical error under the supplied law without establishing the adequacy of the model. Examples: Independent-draw error · Dependent-draw error

When repeated reports state different numbers of pairs, averaging their realized error fractions gives equal weight to reports that state at least one pair. Dividing expected wrong counts by expected stated counts gives more weight to reports that state more pairs. A posterior-reported share instead uses the probabilities supplied to that report. Averaging across reports compares these quantities, and the case-report tool specifies the denominator used in its report.

Domain profiles and joint protection

Domain profiles give each unit a grade for each domain. The following definitions explain how those grades are compared and how joint errors enter the choice of a report.

Domain d; profile matrix G
A substantive reporting coordinate and its grade column. The matrix has units on rows and domains on columns. A common-grade report uses the same column across criteria; a multidomain profile (P2) permits separate columns. Grade 1 denotes the highest reported priority within its own domain. Example: Common grades and domain profiles
Profile tuple; crossed directions
A unit’s ordered domain grades. Tuples such as (1,2) and (2,1) place the first unit higher in one domain and lower in the other. They do not define an overall ranking or an allocation rule. Equal grades within a domain withhold that pair’s direction without asserting equal latent targets. Examples: Common and profile reports · Forty fictional sites
Complete joint state
A set of target values for every included unit and domain in one posterior state. A complete finite law lists each possible state and its probability. Two laws can agree within each domain and differ in how the domains vary together. Row labels on separate simulations are insufficient to recover that dependence. Example: Dependence and joint protection
Fd, Nd, R(G)
In a joint state, Fd counts false strict directions in domain d; a strict assertion at latent equality is false. The reported card discloses Nd pairs in that domain. Its additive expected loss is R(G)=∑dwd{E(Fd)+τd(M−Nd)}/M, with the original M=n(n−1)/2 pair count. This expected cost is distinct from a probability that one or more protected conditions fail. Examples: Separate weighted choices · Reporting resolution
td, α, J(G)
A strict false-direction-proportion threshold, an inclusive joint cap, and the direct union probability J(G)=Pr⁡{at least one d: Fd>tdNd}. The union is formed within the same joint state before its probability is calculated. At Nd=0 the ratio is not defined and the domain’s exceedance event is false. Feasibility requires J(G)≤α, alongside any other declared constraints. Example: Dependence and joint protection
Separable reporting decision
An additive weighted objective whose actual feasible family factors into a Cartesian product of domain choices. Every combination of the complete weighted domain minimizer sets is then optimal in that product. Posterior independence is unnecessary. A zero-weight domain contributes every feasible column to its weighted minimizer set, while retaining its constraints. A joint constraint can prevent the feasible family from factoring. Example: When domain choices separate
Occupied-grade counts (KS,KC)
The numbers of occupied grades in the two domains, rather than a particular site’s profile tuple. With a fixed law, loss and candidate bank, relaxing a budget cannot increase minimum risk. A selected domain’s grade count can nevertheless decrease as reporting detail moves to another domain. Count paths identify the canonical representative when there are tied optima. Example: Posterior uncertainty and reporting resolution
Exact optimizer set; displayed representative
All feasible candidates that attain the same exact minimum loss. The app displays the first in a specified grade order and keeps the other tied reports available. The reported optimum is relative to the candidates included in the search. Examples: Common versus separate columns · Dependence and tied cards
Exact calculations for a finite law
For a complete list of posterior states and probabilities, the app computes losses and tests constraints with exact fractions. A separate assessment is required if the supplied law approximates another posterior. The browser examples accept 2–4 units, 1–2 domains, 1–8 states, and at most 256 distinct profile cards, with at most 256 input columns per domain. The R package also supports assessment with independent draws and MCMC diagnostics. Examples: Joint-law illustration · Specified reversal scenarios
Saved profiles for fictional sites
The forty-site example shows grade tuples, the settings used to choose them, and separate summaries for selection and diagnostics. The grade tuples identify occupied cells and the directions stated in each domain. Rerunning selection or computing new risk probabilities would require the original joint draws. The stored fractions describe the draws used. The diagnostic segment came from the same chain as the selection segment, so it does not provide an independent validation. Example: Profiles of forty fictional sites
