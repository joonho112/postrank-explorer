Terminology and notation

Targets, reporting losses and computation scope

Definitions of posterior quantities, grade reports, disclosure, error measures and report families.

The notation below is used throughout the collection. Numerical interpretation depends on the target orientation, posterior law and eligible report family. Each application specifies these assumptions for its own examples.

Targets and posterior uncertainty

Unit; criterion k
A firm, school, site or other entity included in the comparison population. A criterion is a target on which the report is evaluated. Units outside this population remain unassessed. Examples: Multiple criteria · Supplied summaries
θi
The oriented target for unit i. Larger values denote higher reporting priority. This orientation is specified substantively and need not correspond to better outcomes or greater need. Examples: Two-unit comparison · Alabama counties
Posterior
The distribution of target values conditional on the stated data and model. Reported posterior risk is an expectation under this distribution. Its calculation does not establish calibration in an external population. Examples: Posterior shrinkage · Posterior inputs
πij
The posterior probability of a specified direction, such as Pr⁡(θi>θj). It concerns the joint distribution of two targets; a marginal interval for one unit does not determine it. Examples: Direction probabilities · Interval overlap
eij(k)
The probability that stating i above j is wrong on criterion k. Under the zero-margin weak-error convention, exact equality counts as a wrong strict direction. The forward and reverse errors can therefore sum to more than one when equality has positive probability. Event conventions and comparison margins are specified with the input. Examples: Pairwise decisions · Posterior equality
Marginal interval
A posterior interval for an individual target at a specified probability level. Its level is marginal rather than simultaneous across units. Overlap of two such intervals is distinct from their direction probability. Example: Report-card summaries
Expected rank
The posterior average position of a unit under a specified rank and tie convention. Ordering these averages gives one possible ranking and can define a fixed-order report family. Example: Alternative ranking summaries

Grade reports, disclosure and error

Grade report g
An ordered partition of the included units, with grade 1 at the high-priority end. Different grades state a direction; a shared grade withholds a comparison without asserting equality. Allocation requires an additional decision rule. Examples: Report construction · Allocation objectives
n, M
The number of included units and their M=n(n−1)/2 unordered pairs. Each cross-grade pair contributes one stated direction. Example: Report-family counts
N, s=N/M
The number of stated pairs and the disclosure share. The remaining M−N pairs are withheld. Example: Disclosure from tier sizes
Ek
The posterior expected number of wrong stated directions on criterion k, obtained by summing directional error probabilities over the stated pairs. Joint correctness is a separate probability. Example: Expected and realised errors
Ek/N
The expected wrong share among stated pairs of a fixed report. It is NA: no disclosure when N=0. Example: Error-rate denominators
DRk=Ek/M
The expected wrong count per possible pair. Withholding can reduce this rate by reducing the set of stated directions. The criterion subscript is usually omitted in single-criterion examples. Example: Error-rate denominators
τ
The cost per withheld pair when a wrong stated direction costs one. Weighted risk is Rτ=(Ew+τ(M−N))/M. Example: Reporting risk
λ, ϕ
KRW’s reward for a correct direction and the informed share in their stipulated audience model. The conversion is τ=λ/(1+λ) and ϕ=1−2τ. The positive-cost audience range is 0<λ≤1, 0<τ≤1/2 and 0≤ϕ<1. Equivalence of optimal reports requires the stated common-posterior, tie-free and common-family conditions. Examples: Loss-scale conversion · Equivalent reporting losses

The inequality Ew≤τN holds for a loss-minimising report when its eligible family includes the all-withheld report. It does not apply automatically to an arbitrary fixed report or rule. Shared grades, exact equality of latent values and equal-risk optimisation solutions refer to distinct properties.

Report families and comparisons

Family; grade cap
The set of reports eligible for optimisation. Examples include all reports with at most a specified number of grades and contiguous cuts of a fixed order. An optimum within one order has this restricted scope. Equal order scores may be required to remain together. Examples: Report families · Computation methods
Frontier
The lower convex boundary of expected wrong counts against disclosure in the specified family, allowing randomisation over complete reports. A point on an edge may be unattainable by a single report. Reports optimal at the same τ can have different disclosures. Examples: Error–disclosure frontier · Comparison constraints
ψ
The probability of selecting the higher-disclosure complete report in a mixture of two frontier reports. The resulting expected disclosure describes a randomised reporting rule; it need not correspond to a deterministic intermediate grade vector. Example: Mixtures of reports
wk, Ew=∑kwkEk
Nonnegative criterion error weights summing to one, and the weighted expected wrong count. These weights apply to directional losses. Coefficients of a scalar composite target have a separate role. Examples: Common grades · Composite targets
qk
An expected-error budget, expressed in count form as Ek≤qkN. With no stated directions both sides are zero, although the error share is undefined. Some applications use budgets only to evaluate the displayed report; others use them to restrict selection. Examples: Budget-constrained families · Case-report checks
Δk
The risk of a common report on criterion k minus that criterion’s minimum risk under the same loss and feasible family. Optimisation bounds give an interval when the criterion-specific minimum is not established. Differences between settings or nonnested families need not have this interpretation. Example: Criterion-specific excess risk
Selection belief; evaluation law
The distribution used to select a report and the distribution used to evaluate that fixed report. Evaluation under a different law gives a different expected loss, even when the grade vector is unchanged. Examples: Alternative evaluation laws · Selection-draw evaluation
Joint correctness
The probability that all stated directions are correct together. Pairwise probabilities and expected wrong counts alone do not determine this event probability. Composite targets similarly require the relevant joint-law information. Example: Limits of pairwise probabilities
Simulation error
Numerical uncertainty due to a finite set of posterior draws. Independent draws and dependent chains require different variance calculations and diagnostics. Increasing the number of draws reduces numerical error under the supplied law without establishing the adequacy of the model. Examples: Independent-draw error · Dependent-draw error

Across repeated datasets, the mean of realised error fractions, the ratio of expected wrong and stated counts, and the mean posterior-reported share define different summaries. Their denominators and conditioning must therefore be specified. Averaging across reports illustrates these distinctions, and the case-report tool records the quantities used in its reporting account. None of these summaries alone establishes external calibration.
